Abstract

Delivery and pick-up tasks run from door to door, through rooms, lobbies, sidewalks, and plazas. Indoor and outdoor navigation have been studied apart, and the benchmarks that now join them define door-to-door by where to go: the robot is given a goal and a camera and must find a path. We define it by how to get there. In service use the building map and the destination door are given and a planner plans the route. What remains is to drive it past what the map omits and through doors, with one policy on both sides.

D2D-Nav is built to this definition: a connected indoor and outdoor world with planned routes, unmapped and held-out obstacles, and pre-registered exams, together with warehouse demonstrations and real driving logs read with the same sensors. A world-model driving policy, imitation-trained once in a warehouse and frozen, follows the route but is route-locked: where the route crosses an unmapped obstacle it halts or collides, no residual on its actions passes such a case, and the same policy given a detour route does. A tracking view explains why: the policy's own feedback keeps action-space exploration near the route, whereas shifting the route shifts its equilibrium, provided the route passes through the robot.

DeRoute therefore edits the route: LiDAR returns binned in the route frame feed a small head that predicts a bounded lateral deviation, an ego-anchored executor hands the rectified route to the driving policy, and an evidence-gated prior keeps the deviation at zero where nothing blocks the route. The driving policy with DeRoute passes 135 of 144 pre-registered cases and 16 of 17 cases across obstacle categories withheld from training.

The benchmark

Navigation has two halves, where to go and how to get there. NavVerse and I2O2I-Nav define door-to-door by where to go and leave the driving to a follower that is not under test. D2D-Nav gives the planned route and the sensors a service robot carries, a LiDAR for where things are and six cameras for what they are, and tests the driving: doors as narrow as 1.8 m, objects absent from the map, and categories withheld from training.

The world is a city block exported from Unreal Engine to Isaac Sim. An outdoor plaza with streets and sidewalks and three indoor scenes, a home, a hospital lobby, and an office lobby, are joined by doors. A planner computes natural routes on the static map, which omits the obstacles, so it neither knows nor avoids them.

Route-locked

The driving policy follows the route it reads. Where the planned route crosses an object the map omits, it halts or collides. A residual on its actions, trained with the recipe of COMPASS, restores route following in the new world but passes none of these cases, and the same policy given a detour route passes.

A tracking view explains why. The policy's own feedback pulls any change in its actions back toward the route, so exploration in action space stays near the route. Shifting the route shifts the point the policy tracks, provided the route passes through the robot.

DeRoute

DeRoute edits the route, not the action. LiDAR returns binned in the frame of the planned route feed a deviation head of 27.5k parameters, the only trained part. Its bounded output sets a lateral deviation, which an ego-anchored executor turns into the rectified route that the driving policy reads. An evidence-gated prior keeps the deviation at zero where nothing blocks the route, so the policy keeps to unobstructed routes and through doors.

The driving policy itself, a world model over six cameras and LiDAR imitation-trained in the warehouse, is never updated.

Results

All methods run the same exams with the same judge. A case passes if the robot ends within 0.5 m of the goal within 60 s, without contact and inside the drivable area. DeRoute passed 135 cases in each of three final-exam runs, and we do not rank differences of fewer than four cases.

Real driving logs

GhostClip logs a slow ground robot with the sensor layout of our base, six cameras and a 31-beam LiDAR. Each frame gives a method its sensors, its speed, and the next 20 m of the driven path as the route, and is read open loop against the motion over the next 0.5 s.

Fine-tuned on the training logs, the base predicts the turn rate better than repeating the last motion, 0.021 against 0.030 rad/s, and on turns 0.040 against 0.057, from 0.204 with simulation alone.
